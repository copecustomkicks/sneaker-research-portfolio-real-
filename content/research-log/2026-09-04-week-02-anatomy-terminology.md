## Objectives for the week

- Build a working map of the shoe's anatomy before moving into materials and manufacturing research.
- Identify the major upper, internal, sole, traction, and joining components of an athletic shoe and each one's primary function.
- Distinguish commonly confused terms — vamp vs. toe box, heel counter vs. heel clip, midsole vs. insert, and others.
- Understand how Strobel, board-lasted, knit, cut-and-sew, and bonded constructions change what is physically present in the shoe.

## Activities completed

- Built a five-system mental model for organizing the shoe: upper assembly, internal/lasting structure, cushioning/structural sole, ground-contact system, and joining system, instead of the simpler upper/midsole/outsole split.
- Produced master component tables covering thirty components across those systems, each with its location, primary function, and typical loading. These are now live on the [sneaker anatomy page](/anatomy).
- Documented ten pairs of commonly confused terminology: vamp vs. toe box, toe box vs. toe cap, quarter vs. eyestay, collar vs. heel counter, heel counter vs. heel clip, Strobel vs. lasting board, sockliner vs. midsole, midsole vs. foam insert, plate vs. shank, and outsole vs. rubber coverage.
- Documented six construction architectures — Strobel lasting, board lasting, cemented construction, knit/one-piece upper, cut-and-sew upper, and multi-layer bonded upper — and how each changes which discrete parts are physically present.
- Worked through ten ambiguous-classification cases (TPU cage, carbon-fiber plate, foam insert inside the carrier, dual-density midsole, integrated knit support zones, rubberized toe coating, midsole sidewall extension, a visible stiff rearfoot shell, absent outsole rubber, thick removable internal foam) and set a consistent classification rule for each.
- Built an anatomy audit template to use during intact-shoe inspection and again after teardown.
- Read manufacturing-process detail from a practitioner reference (*How Shoes Are Made*, Wade Motawi) covering last measurement terminology, cold cement and vulcanized sole-attachment construction, and footbed/sockliner construction and materials — ahead of the Phase 3/4 schedule, but directly useful to this week's anatomy and lasting work, so it went straight into the [processes](/processes) and [anatomy](/anatomy) pages rather than waiting.
- Added a standard last-measurement vocabulary (stick length, bottom length, toe spring, heel lift, and the ball/waist/instep/heel girths and widths) and the last-gauge quality-control method to the last-selection process record.
- Added a Last entry to the anatomy reference now that there is a sourced basis for it, and enriched the sockliner entry with real footbed construction routes (die-cut, compression-molded, poured) and materials (EVA, poured polyurethane).
- Added two real material records — EVA and poured-polyurethane footbed foam — to the materials library, the first entries since it was reset to empty.

## Methods used

- Terminology cross-check across two footwear-construction patents (US11178934B2, US20160302519A1), two further midsole/Strobel patents (US12114726B2, US20200170340A1), a specialist shoe-parts reference (Shoemakers Academy), and two academic theses plus one industry/university presentation.
- Built the component classification hierarchy by separating a component's physical role and location from its material and manufacturing process — a rule carried forward into all future component documentation.
- Reviewed photographed pages from *How Shoes Are Made* covering cold cement construction, vulcanized construction, last measurement, last-gauge quality control, and footbed construction and materials.

## Key findings

- [Published] Strobel and board lasting are functionally distinct bottom-closure methods: a Strobel is a flexible component sewn to the upper perimeter, while a lasting board is a stiffer platform — confirmed across the patent sources and the Shoemakers Academy reference.
- [Published] Traditional upper terminology (vamp, quarter, eyestay) names functional regions, not necessarily separate cut panels. In knit or bonded uppers those regions can be integrated into one continuous structure.
- [Published] A component name should describe physical role and location first. Material names such as TPU, carbon fiber, rubber, or PEBA do not by themselves identify the component.
- [Assumption] Category-specific architecture patterns (running, basketball, training, lifestyle) are useful hypotheses for what to expect on a given shoe, not fixed rules. These need to be checked against real teardown observations, not assumed.
- [Published] Cold cement and vulcanized construction differ in exactly when and how the outsole is attached, not just in adhesive choice: cold cement bonds two independently completed assemblies (upper, sole unit) together at the end, while vulcanized construction builds the outsole up in uncured pieces directly on the lasted upper and cures the whole thing in an oven — which is also why a vulcanized last must be aluminum and why EVA foam can't be used as its midsole (the cure heat would destroy it).
- [Published] A footbed's lifespan is governed mainly by a foam's resistance to compression set, not its initial softness — a cheap, overly soft foam can pack out within days even though it feels good in the store.

## Engineering decisions

- Adopted the five-system mental model as the organizing framework for the rest of the anatomy and teardown work.
- Set a documentation rule: label anything not visible or verifiable from an intact shoe as "not externally verifiable" rather than guessing, and defer materials chemistry and manufacturing-process detail to the materials (Phase 3) and manufacturing (Phase 4) research — with the exception of anatomy- and lasting-relevant process detail that arrived this week already sourced, which went in now rather than being held back arbitrarily.

## Challenges and failures

- Public terminology is inconsistent across sources — terms like Strobel, slip, California, insole, and counter are used differently by different manufacturers and references. Resolved by prioritizing the observed physical construction over any single source's label.
- Several details — plate extent, internal foam geometry, heel-counter geometry, exact lasting construction, adhesive coverage — cannot be confirmed from an intact shoe. These are flagged as open questions for the teardown phase rather than guessed at.

## Reflection

This week reframed the shoe as an interconnected system rather than a list of memorized part names — the anatomy vocabulary only becomes useful once it is tied to function, location, and how the construction method changes what is actually present. I am still unsure how much the category-specific architecture patterns (running vs. basketball vs. training) will hold up once real teardown observations are available.

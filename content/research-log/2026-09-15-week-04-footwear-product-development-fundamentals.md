## Objectives for the week

- Learn the footwear developer's role in product development, distinct from design and product line management (PLM).
- Understand the last's role in fit and construction, and the upper/midsole/outsole versus upper/bottom-unit ways of describing a shoe.
- Learn injection-molded, compression-molded, and die-cut EVA midsole manufacturing methods and their tradeoffs.
- Learn stack height and heel-to-toe drop as distinct terms.
- Practice component labeling and callout conventions — including overlays versus underlays — and see how callouts feed the bill of materials and tech pack.
- Apply the vocabulary by producing component callout studies for two real sneakers, ahead of the course's reverse tech pack assignment.

## Activities completed

- Attended class sessions on September 15 and 17 covering what makes footwear product development successful: fit and function, profitability and manufacturability (a single successful sample doesn't prove factory-scale repeatability), sustainability, and timing/design intent for launches and marketing.
- Studied the distinct roles in a product-development team: the designer sets the visual concept and intent; the developer translates that intent into a workable, producible product and manages the factory relationship; product line management (PLM in these notes means the product-line-management role, not product-lifecycle-management software) connects the product to the market, tracks trends, and keeps the team aligned with the brief.
- Studied the last as the three-dimensional form that sets the starting point for fit and shapes the upper pattern and the upper-to-bottom-unit relationship. Changing a last can require changes across the whole shoe, not just a local fix. Class examples included athletic, basketball, cleat, athlete-specific, and split-toe lasts; last material must suit the manufacturing process (e.g., metal lasts for the heat used in vulcanized construction). Whether related models share a last is a hypothesis to verify, not something to assume from similar appearance.
- Reviewed the upper/midsole/outsole breakdown alongside the upper/bottom-unit breakdown as two different but valid ways of describing the same shoe — neither implies every visible sidewall is its own separate midsole. Upper: containment, support, protection, breathability, appearance (textiles, synthetics, leather). Midsole: cushioning, stability, flexibility, support, shock absorption, energy return — dependent on material and geometry together. Outsole: ground contact, traction, durability, flexibility, and underfoot feel.
- Worked through outsole/bottom-unit examples: zonal outsoling (placing rubber only where contact/wear demands it), herringbone traction (a pattern whose features can deform with use), the Air Jordan 1 as a cupsole example (the visible rubber sidewall wraps up around the upper — it is not "exposed midsole foam"; the tradeoff is added weight for a specific look and craftsmanship, not a rule against cupsoles in performance footwear), classic Converse as a vulcanized-construction and foxing-tape example (heat compatibility matters for both last and upper materials), and the Nike Roshe as a unitsole example (consolidating bottom-unit functions simplifies construction, but performance still depends on compound, geometry, and intended use).
- Studied midsole materials and manufacturing methods. EVA, Phylon, PU, and proprietary compounds were introduced as starting vocabulary, not a full description of density, processing, or performance. Compression set — residual deformation after compression and recovery — is a behavior to evaluate, not a desired function; it's why an older shoe can start feeling "dead." Softness alone doesn't establish energy return, durability, or suitability for a given athlete.
  - **Injection molded EVA**: an EVA formulation with a blowing agent is injected into a mold and expands; tooling has to account for that expansion, which can make fine detail harder to hold. Relatively low material waste (losses mainly from runners/sprues). A small-mold example illustrated expansion in class — that example, not a fixed one-third-size ratio, is what should carry over, since actual expansion depends on the formulation and process.
  - **Compression molded EVA**: an EVA preform goes into a mold under heat and pressure. Presented in class as offering sharper detail and easier part-combination than injection molding, with mold venting as part of process control and a separate stockfitting/assembly step often needed to attach a rubber outsole. The class tradeoff was "greater detail, higher cost" — useful for understanding the lecture example, but actual cost depends on tooling, labor, and quantity.
  - **Die-cut EVA**: cut from flat sheet stock rather than molded to shape. Relatively low tooling investment, more cutting waste, more labor, and real shaping limitations. The class's sheet-stock example was described as hard underfoot — that's specific to that example, not a property of all EVA sheet stock.
- Learned stack height (construction thickness at a specified point, e.g., heel or forefoot) and heel-to-toe drop (the difference between heel and forefoot stack height) as two distinct terms. **Correction to my own notes**: I had written "heel height minus forefoot height = stack height" — that's actually the definition of drop, not stack height. Zero-drop footwear is a geometry choice; it does not mean zero cushioning.
- Studied component-labeling conventions: name a part by its location and construction, not by color (color helps locate a part in a photo but doesn't establish material, function, or layering). Worked through the overlay/underlay distinction (an overlay sits over another component, an underlay sits beneath one) using real examples — a back-tab overlay, a tip-area component, a quarter/eyestay overlay, an upper heel overlay, and a lateral lower heel overlay — including the specific question of whether a continuous tip should just be called a tip rather than an added "tip overlay." The right label follows the shoe's actual panel boundaries and layering, not a default word choice.
- Discussed visible parts versus hidden construction: internal items such as a lining, heel counter, toe reinforcement, strobel/lasting layer, sockliner, and internal cushioning are categories to investigate on a real shoe from an exterior photo, not a claim that all of them have been confirmed present in either shoe I studied. Confirming hidden structure needs an internal view, disassembly, or reliable model-specific documentation.
- Connected component labeling to the bill of materials (the class's "buy sheet" example: an unlabeled or omitted component may simply not get purchased) and to the tech pack — the brief, blank drawings, and colorway drawings that actually communicate a build to a factory. A polished picture alone doesn't carry enough information to manufacture a shoe.
- Noted duty rates as a research item raised in class (logo placement, performance story, and intuitive design were the other upper-design considerations discussed) — this week's work did not establish any specific tariff classification or rate; that's flagged as a follow-up, not something I looked into yet.
- Completed two component callout studies applying this week's vocabulary to real shoes, traced and rendered by me in Adobe Illustrator with my own numbered callouts added on top (see Evidence and artifacts below).
- Began preparing for the course's reverse tech pack assignment — working backward from a finished, cut-apart shoe to a full technical package (or an original design, under the course's alternative option). This week's labeling work is early groundwork for that, not the completed assignment.

## Engineering decisions

- A compelling sketch is only a starting point — for my own project, every component and material choice needs to be tied to fit, function, and a feasible construction process, not just appearance. A visually simple shoe can still be hard to develop well, since clean execution leaves nowhere to hide inconsistencies.
- Label components by location and construction, not by color, going forward — carrying the class's naming convention into my own anatomy and component work.
- Treat "does this shoe share a last with that one" and "is this internal component actually present" as hypotheses requiring verification (a real inspection, disassembly, or reliable documentation), not conclusions from a photo or similar appearance.
- Keep the stack-height/drop distinction and the corrected definition (drop = heel stack height − forefoot stack height) as the definitions I use from here forward.

## Reflection

This week was less about new anatomy vocabulary and more about how that vocabulary gets used in an actual development process — naming things precisely enough that a factory, a developer, and a designer are all talking about the same part. The stack-height/drop mix-up in my own notes was a useful reminder to double-check definitions rather than assume I'd absorbed them correctly the first time. Doing the two callout studies made the labeling rules concrete in a way the lecture examples alone didn't — deciding whether something is a tip or a "tip overlay," or whether a visible rubber wrap is a cupsole feature rather than exposed midsole foam, forces a level of precision I'll need for my own project's component work later.

## Objectives for the week

- Move from documenting a shoe to confirming, troubleshooting, and communicating it toward production.
- Practice diagnosing BOM and specification errors systematically rather than just flagging anomalies.

## Activities completed

- Learned product confirmation — verifying specs, materials, construction, tooling, and color accurately represent the final product before commercialization — and product codes for tracking through development and manufacturing.
- Studied commercialization as the shift from a working prototype to manufacturing that reliably repeats the confirmed specification, reinforcing Week 05's lesson that sample success and production readiness are different claims.
- Learned the five-question troubleshooting framework for manufacturing errors: what's incorrect, what was specified, why the discrepancy likely occurred, what needs to change, and how to prevent recurrence — systematic diagnosis over arbitrary fixes.
- Completed a BOM error-analysis exercise, comparing a bill of materials against a finished shoe for discrepancies in component descriptions, colors, materials, branding treatment, and construction method (see Evidence and artifacts). The clearest example: a webbing logo specified as embroidered when the actual branding was a separate raised/molded element — same logo, color, and location, but the wrong construction method still makes the BOM wrong.
- Reinforced the course's "think like a developer" standard: flag a specific, real inconsistency between spec and product, identify the likely correction, and communicate it precisely — not flag anything that merely looks unusual.
- Expanded on wear testing: beyond fit and function, it surfaces durability, component failure, material wear, outsole abrasion, and usage patterns across testers, letting developers tell an isolated issue from a systemic one.
- Covered customization's effect on manufacturing complexity, lean manufacturing (cutting non-value-adding steps — excess process steps, difficult material handling, rework, defects), and advanced manufacturing (new processes still have to meet quality, repeatability, cost, speed, and scalability requirements, not just be newer).
- Studied factory communication: specific, concise, technically accurate, visually supported, and structured to separate current condition, the error, the desired condition, the requested change, and open questions — since vague instructions across language and cultural differences get expensive.
- Studied how a developer evaluates a designer's requested change before it reaches the factory: timing, cost, tooling, materials, construction feasibility, existing samples, testing, commercialization status, and production schedule.

## Reflection

This week made development feel closer to real industry work than any prior week — less about learning vocabulary, more about evaluating discrepancies and reducing uncertainty before production. The BOM exercise was the clearest example: a component can have the right logo, color, and location and still be wrong if the construction method doesn't match. That's a precision standard I want to carry into my own documentation later.

## Next steps

- Carry the five-question troubleshooting framework into any BOM or spec work for my own project.
- Apply the factory-communication structure (current condition, error, desired condition, requested change, open questions) to any revision notes I write later.

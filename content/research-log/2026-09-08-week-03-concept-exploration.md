## Objectives for the week

- Start moving from "what are a sneaker's parts and what do they do" (Week 02) toward "what should those parts accomplish for a specific user."
- Identify a small number of plausible directions for the eventual prototype, realistic for a first-time sneaker maker.
- Review literature relevant to each candidate direction to replace assumptions with evidence where possible.
- Decide how much to narrow the project scope this early, given how little I still know about materials, manufacturing, and construction.

## Activities completed

- Outlined three candidate product directions and worked through the reasoning for each rather than picking one outright:
  - **Achilles-conscious running shoe.** A running shoe for a runner with Achilles tightness or a preference for geometry that could alter Achilles loading. Areas of interest: heel-to-toe geometry, rocker geometry, midsole stiffness and deformation, load paths, plates or structural elements, and a possible 3D-printed or lattice midsole. Strongest mechanical-engineering angle and the clearest 3D-printing opportunity, but framing it around an injury quickly turns it into a medical/rehabilitative claim, and actually showing reduced Achilles loading would need biomechanical validation well beyond building a functional shoe.
  - **Supportive casual/lifestyle sneaker.** An everyday sneaker for someone who wants more deliberate underfoot support without the shoe looking orthopedic. Far more realistic as a first complete build, and it would let me work through nearly every component from the Week 02 anatomy reference (upper, vamp, quarter, eyestay, tongue, heel counter, sockliner, lasting system, midsole, outsole). Main risk is reading as less technically ambitious unless the support/sole system is a deliberately engineered variable, and "arch support" framing has its own smaller version of the medical-claim problem.
  - **Performance-lifestyle / walking shoe.** A visually clean shoe for extended walking and standing, with a more engineered midsole or support system and room for a 3D-printed element. Positioned as a middle ground — more technically interesting than the casual concept, easier to validate and build than the Achilles concept.
- Compared the three directions qualitatively (engineering depth, first-build feasibility, 3D-printing opportunity, validation difficulty, industry relevance) as a way to organize the tradeoffs — not a scored decision matrix, and not a final ranking.
- Reviewed five sources spanning Achilles tendon loading, arch-support pressure distribution, casual-footwear comfort, and 3D-printed lattice midsoles, and connected each one's findings to the candidate directions above (full list and implications under Sources reviewed).
- Decided not to select a final concept this week. All three are carried forward as live options, now with more specific design variables attached to each.
- Started my Footwear Product Development course, which runs in parallel to this project. Week 1 of the course covers pre-development work, the footwear product-development process, footwear basics (lasts, uppers, midsoles, outsoles), lasts and shell patterns, tooling, development handoffs, global development teams, component labeling, and the distinction between PLM and product development. Deliverables were an introduction/favorite-footwear discussion, a labeled photo of a favorite athletic shoe's components, and Quiz 1.

## Key findings

- Heel-to-toe geometry is a legitimate, measurable design variable for the Achilles concept: in a controlled running study, an 18 mm heel lift reduced peak Achilles tendon force from 2,710 N to 2,382 N (about 12%) and peak tendon strain from 7.4% to 5.5%; a 12 mm lift did not produce a significant change. The study tested running only, in ten women, so it does not establish 18 mm as the right lift for this project or say anything about walking — it establishes that lift height is worth comparing directly rather than assuming a value.
- A more minimal or "natural" shoe does not automatically reduce Achilles tendon demand. An acute-loading comparison in sixteen male habitual rearfoot-strike runners found that switching to a minimalist shoe increased Achilles tendon force and loading rate relative to a conventional shoe. Any Achilles-focused brief needs to measure loading directly and account for the wearer's existing footwear habits rather than assume less shoe means less tendon demand.
- Arch-support material hardness had no measurable effect on plantar pressure distribution. A one-month randomized crossover study comparing two custom 3D-printed arch supports of different hardness against a prefabricated support (twelve healthy volunteers) found all three redistributed pressure toward the midfoot similarly; hardness made no difference. The 3D-printed supports scored better on comfort despite no pressure-distribution advantage. That separates two variables I'd been treating as one — shape and fit drive pressure distribution, comfort is a separate measurement, and custom printing doesn't by itself redistribute pressure better than an off-the-shelf part.
- Footwear comfort extends past midsole cushioning. A comparison of commercial casual shoes found uncomfortable pairs produced higher peak pressures, with comfortable shoes showing more contact area under the midfoot and uncomfortable shoes loading the top of the foot more heavily. For the casual/lifestyle concept, comfort work should include tongue padding, lacing pressure, and upper fit, not just the sole, and should be scored by foot region rather than a single overall number.
- Internal lattice geometry measurably changes midsole pressure behavior. A finite-element comparison of 60°, 75°, and non-auxetic 90° lattice midsoles found the 60° auxetic design reduced peak plantar pressure by roughly 20–55% relative to the 90° baseline across walking and running conditions. That gives a 3D-printed midsole concept a concrete starting design variable (lattice angle), though the study evaluated pressure and deformation in a diabetic-footwear context — it doesn't establish athletic comfort, durability, or performance, which this project would still need to test.

## Engineering decisions

- Keep the final shoe concept intentionally open after Week 03 rather than choosing now — this is scope management, not indecision, since committing before understanding materials, manufacturing, benchmarking, and construction risks designing around bad assumptions.
- Treat first-build feasibility as a criterion alongside engineering depth and industry relevance, not an afterthought to whichever concept is most technically interesting.
- Avoid unsupported clinical or therapeutic claims in any concept framing from here forward.
- Do not assume a lower-profile or "minimalist" shoe is inherently gentler on the Achilles tendon — treat that as something to measure, not a default.
- If the Achilles concept advances, plan to compare heel-lift heights experimentally rather than adopt 18 mm by default; that value came from one running study in a small, all-female sample.
- If the casual/lifestyle concept advances, prioritize arch shape and fit over material hardness, and score comfort by foot region instead of one overall rating.
- If a 3D-printed midsole advances under either the Achilles or performance-lifestyle concept, start with controlled lattice-angle samples (60° as a reference point) before committing to a full printed sole.
- This concept-development work is ahead of the roadmap's Phase 5 schedule, similar to how Motawi manufacturing content was pulled into Week 02 early — it goes in now because it is directly useful, not because Phase 5 has formally started.

## Sources reviewed

- Farris, D. J., Buckeridge, E., Trewartha, G., & McGuigan, M. P. (2012). The effects of orthotic heel lifts on Achilles tendon force and strain during running. *Journal of Applied Biomechanics, 28*(5), 511–519.
  Finding: In ten women running with heel lifts, an 18 mm lift reduced estimated peak Achilles tendon force from 2,710 N to 2,382 N (about 12%) and peak tendon strain from 7.4% to 5.5%; a 12 mm lift did not produce a significant reduction. Running only — this study does not address walking.
  Project implication: Heel elevation is worth carrying forward as a measurable design variable for an Achilles-focused concept. I'd compare elevations experimentally before selecting one; this study does not establish 18 mm as the right heel-to-toe drop for my sneaker.
- Zhang, X., Deng, L., Yang, Y., Li, L., & Fu, W. (2020). Acute shoe effects on Achilles tendon loading in runners with a habitual rearfoot strike pattern. *Gait & Posture, 82*, 322–328.
  Finding: In sixteen male habitual rearfoot-strike runners, switching immediately to a minimalist shoe increased Achilles tendon force and loading rate compared with a conventional shoe. The study measured acute loading changes, not injury outcomes.
  Project implication: This rules out assuming a more minimal or "natural" shoe automatically reduces tendon demand. For an Achilles-focused brief, I'd evaluate the shoe's effect on loading directly and account for the wearer's existing footwear habits.
- Channasanon, S., et al. (2023). 3D-printed medial arch supports of varying hardness versus a prefabricated arch support on plantar pressure: A 1-month randomized crossover study in healthy volunteers. *Prosthetics and Orthotics International*.
  Finding: In twelve healthy volunteers, all three supports (two 3D-printed, one prefabricated) redistributed average pressure toward the midfoot and away from the forefoot and heel after one month of use. Hardness had no apparent effect on pressure distribution. The 3D-printed supports scored better on comfort despite no pressure-distribution advantage over the prefabricated support.
  Project implication: I'd prioritize arch shape and fit alongside material selection, and measure comfort separately from pressure. The evidence doesn't support assuming softer material — or custom printing alone — produces better pressure relief.
- Jordan, C., Payton, C. J., & Bartlett, R. M. (1997). Perceived comfort and pressure distribution in casual footwear. *Clinical Biomechanics, 12*(3), S5.
  Finding: Across a set of commercial casual shoes, uncomfortable footwear produced higher peak pressures. Comfortable shoes had greater contact area beneath the midfoot, while uncomfortable shoes also imposed greater maximum force on the top of the foot.
  Project implication: This expands the comfort concept beyond midsole cushioning. I'd include tongue padding, lacing pressure, and upper fit in the design, then collect comfort ratings by foot region so an overall score doesn't hide a specific pressure hotspot.
- Zhang, et al. (2024). Pressure-reducing design of a 3D-printed diabetic shoe midsole utilizing auxetic lattice structure. *Applied Sciences, 14*(12), 5291.
  Finding: A finite-element comparison of 60° and 75° auxetic lattice midsoles against a 90° non-auxetic design, under walking and running conditions, found the 60° design produced the largest peak plantar pressure reduction (roughly 20–55% versus the 90° baseline), demonstrating that internal lattice geometry is a functional design variable.
  Project implication: This gives a printed-midsole concept a concrete engineering question — how lattice geometry affects compression and pressure distribution — and a starting reference point (60°). I'd begin with controlled lattice samples before printing a complete sole. The transferable contribution is the design-and-testing method; the paper doesn't establish athletic comfort, durability, or performance for my eventual sneaker.

## Reflection

The biggest outcome this week was realizing that picking a shoe too early would probably work against the project — and the literature review reinforced that rather than resolving it. I started the week thinking fairly directly about individual shoe ideas, and both the concept comparison and the sources kept surfacing how many of the real decisions depend on knowledge and testing I don't have yet. The Achilles-running concept looks more concrete now that heel-lift height has real numbers behind it, but the minimalist-shoe finding was a reminder not to assume any geometry change helps without measuring it. The casual/supportive concept got more specific too: arch support turns out to be more about shape and fit than material hardness, and comfort is a whole-shoe question, not just a cushioning question. The lattice-midsole paper gave the 3D-printing idea an actual starting variable (angle) instead of just an attractive-sounding process. None of this selected a concept — if anything it gave me more to test within each one — but I ended the week with a sharper, evidence-backed sense of the design space.

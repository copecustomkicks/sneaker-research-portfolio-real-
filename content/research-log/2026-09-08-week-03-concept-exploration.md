## Objectives for the week

- Start moving from "what are a sneaker's parts and what do they do" (Week 02) toward "what should those parts accomplish for a specific user."
- Identify a small number of plausible directions for the eventual prototype, realistic for a first-time sneaker maker.
- Decide how much to narrow the project scope this early, given how little I still know about materials, manufacturing, and construction.

## Activities completed

- Outlined three candidate product directions and worked through the reasoning for each rather than picking one outright:
  - **Achilles-conscious running shoe.** A running shoe for a runner with Achilles tightness or a preference for geometry that could alter Achilles loading. Areas of interest: heel-to-toe geometry, rocker geometry, midsole stiffness and deformation, load paths, plates or structural elements, and a possible 3D-printed or lattice midsole. Strongest mechanical-engineering angle and the clearest 3D-printing opportunity, but framing it around an injury quickly turns it into a medical/rehabilitative claim, and actually showing reduced Achilles loading would need biomechanical validation well beyond building a functional shoe.
  - **Supportive casual/lifestyle sneaker.** An everyday sneaker for someone who wants more deliberate underfoot support without the shoe looking orthopedic. Far more realistic as a first complete build, and it would let me work through nearly every component from the Week 02 anatomy reference (upper, vamp, quarter, eyestay, tongue, heel counter, sockliner, lasting system, midsole, outsole). Main risk is reading as less technically ambitious unless the support/sole system is a deliberately engineered variable, and "arch support" framing has its own smaller version of the medical-claim problem.
  - **Performance-lifestyle / walking shoe.** A visually clean shoe for extended walking and standing, with a more engineered midsole or support system and room for a 3D-printed element. Positioned as a middle ground — more technically interesting than the casual concept, easier to validate and build than the Achilles concept.
- Compared the three directions qualitatively (engineering depth, first-build feasibility, 3D-printing opportunity, validation difficulty, industry relevance) as a way to organize the tradeoffs — not a scored decision matrix, and not a final ranking.
- Decided not to select a final concept this week. All three are carried forward as live options.
- Started my Footwear Product Development course, which runs in parallel to this project. Week 1 of the course covers pre-development work, the footwear product-development process, footwear basics (lasts, uppers, midsoles, outsoles), lasts and shell patterns, tooling, development handoffs, global development teams, component labeling, and the distinction between PLM and product development. Deliverables were an introduction/favorite-footwear discussion, a labeled photo of a favorite athletic shoe's components, and Quiz 1.

## Key considerations / research

- [Assumption] 3D printing could be useful for a midsole or support structure — rapid iteration, lattice geometry, zonal stiffness, and a direct CAD-to-physical link — but nothing was committed to. It should be justified by the design problem, not used because the tool is available.
- [Project conclusion] Terms like "Achilles injury," "poor arch support," "injury prevention," or "rehabilitation" can turn a footwear concept into a clinical or therapeutic claim without meaning to. Going forward, concept language should describe intended mechanical behavior (geometry, support, loading, comfort) rather than implied medical outcomes.
- I did not do formal literature research this week on Achilles biomechanics, arch support, or plantar pressure. That reading is deferred until closer to concept selection (see sources below).
- Rough mapping of possible user needs back to Week 02 vocabulary: rearfoot control involves the heel counter, heel clip, quarter, and midsole sidewalls; underfoot support involves the sockliner, footbed, and midsole geometry; fit and containment involve the vamp, quarter, eyestay, lacing, and tongue. No numerical targets were attached to any of these yet.

## Engineering decisions

- Keep the final shoe concept intentionally open after Week 03 rather than choosing now — this is scope management, not indecision, since committing before understanding materials, manufacturing, benchmarking, and construction risks designing around bad assumptions.
- Treat first-build feasibility as a criterion alongside engineering depth and industry relevance, not an afterthought to whichever concept is most technically interesting.
- Avoid unsupported clinical or therapeutic claims in any concept framing from here forward.
- This concept-development work is ahead of the roadmap's Phase 5 schedule, similar to how Motawi manufacturing content was pulled into Week 02 early — it goes in now because it is directly useful, not because Phase 5 has formally started.

## Reflection

The biggest outcome this week was realizing that picking a shoe too early would probably work against the project. I started the week thinking fairly directly about individual shoe ideas, and the process kept surfacing how many of the real decisions depend on knowledge I don't have yet. The Achilles-running concept showed how fast an interesting mechanical problem turns into a validation and medical-framing problem. The casual/supportive concept showed that a much more achievable shoe can still be technically meaningful if the support and sole system are deliberately engineered rather than an afterthought. The performance-lifestyle concept sits between the two. I ended the week with a better sense of the design space, without narrowing it prematurely.

## Next steps

- Continue the Footwear Product Development course.
- Continue benchmark shoe inspection and teardown work.
- Learn more about materials and manufacturing before fixing a construction strategy.
- Review the follow-up literature on Achilles loading, arch support, comfort, plantar pressure, and 3D-printed midsoles.
- Determine what fabrication equipment and methods are realistically available.
- Later, compare the three concepts more formally using criteria such as engineering depth, manufacturability, testability, cost, time, first-build feasibility, and industry relevance.
- Delay the final product brief until these inputs give a stronger basis for choosing.

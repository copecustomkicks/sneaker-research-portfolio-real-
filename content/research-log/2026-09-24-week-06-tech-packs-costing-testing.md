## Objectives for the week

- Move from component identification into the documentation, decision-making, and testing processes footwear developers actually use.
- Understand how development work is sequenced against a product creation calendar.
- Learn what a complete tech pack contains and why ambiguity in it is treated as a documentation failure.
- Learn how the bill of materials, tooling, and existing-tooling reuse affect product economics.
- Learn basic footwear costing contributors and the sample-room-versus-mass-production distinction.
- Learn the role and limits of material testing and wear testing in development.

## Activities completed

- Learned that footwear development runs on a structured product creation calendar rather than a purely design-driven process — specific activities have to happen at specific stages for a design to move from concept to samples, testing, commercialization, and mass production. The developer tracks product details, cost, materials, tooling, samples, testing, revisions, and deadlines simultaneously as the link between design intent and manufacturing execution.
- Studied the footwear tech pack as the communication document that turns a two-dimensional design into factory-usable instructions. A complete tech pack can include: marketing/product brief, blank or identification CAD, component callout CAD, construction-detail callout CAD, material CAD, bill of materials, shell pattern, tooling drawing, rough cost breakdown, development/workback calendar, wear-test plan, and a factory summary of product strengths and development challenges. Main lesson: a good tech pack minimizes ambiguity — if the factory has to keep asking what was intended, the documentation wasn't detailed enough.
- Extended the last → shell pattern → upper → tooling relationship from Week 04: the shell pattern is the two-dimensional pattern framework used to build upper components that will conform to the three-dimensional last. Tooling mainly refers to midsole/outsole molds. A last and tooling are not the same thing — a plastic last doesn't inherently require a new mold the way a molded bottom-unit component does. Reusing existing midsole/outsole tooling on a new model avoids major new tooling investment, shifting the cost focus to materials, labor, and overhead instead.
- Developed a stronger understanding of the bill of materials as more than a parts list — it has to correctly specify component names, material specifications, color specifications, construction/application methods, embellishments, and vendor/supplier information. A small BOM error can get a factory to manufacture something visibly different from what was intended.
- Studied embellishments and application methods: identifying that a logo exists isn't enough — how it's physically applied (embroidery, printing, heat transfer, molded/3D branding, patches, attached components) changes the materials, tooling, labor, and process required, even for two logos that look visually identical.
- Introduced basic footwear costing: upper materials, bottom-unit materials, labor, overhead, tooling, embellishments, and construction complexity as cost contributors, and the Cost Breakdown (CBD) as the tool for understanding where a shoe's cost originates. A developer has to evaluate not just whether a design is technically possible, but whether it can be manufactured at the target cost.
- Worked through a sample-room-versus-mass-production exercise: a sample room can absorb heavy manual labor, hand finishing, individual adjustment, slower processes, small-quantity specialty materials, and techniques that need unusually skilled operators — none of which necessarily scale to producing thousands of pairs. Reinforced that successfully making one shoe does not prove it can be successfully mass-produced.
- Covered material testing — evaluating materials against the demands of their actual intended application (durability, repeated use, environmental exposure, flexibility, adhesion) rather than appearance alone — and wear testing, where real consumers or testers use the shoe under real-world conditions, commonly for about 4–8 weeks, often in sample sizes and sometimes in understated or flooded colorways (e.g., all black) so an unreleased product attracts less attention. Wear testing can evaluate fit, comfort, function, durability, material behavior, outsole wear, upper performance, and user behavior, frequency, and environment. Learned that highly confidential or visually distinctive prototypes may need to stay in internal testing rather than going out to external wear testers.
- Began shifting from evaluating footwear as a consumer or designer to evaluating it as a developer: can the factory understand exactly how this should be built, can it be produced repeatedly and at the required volume, what does the construction cost, can the proposed materials survive their intended use, does a sample-room technique scale to production, and what needs to be tested before commercialization.

## Engineering decisions

- Adopt the developer's question set, not the consumer's or designer's, when evaluating any of my own candidate concept directions going forward: can it be built as intended, produced repeatedly and at volume, costed realistically, and does the construction survive its intended use.
- Treat "worked once as a sample" and "can be mass-produced" as two separate claims — relevant to any construction or finishing choice I consider for my own prototype later.
- Treat how an embellishment or logo is physically applied, not just that it exists, as part of any future bill-of-materials or construction documentation for my own project.

## Reflection

The real shift this week was realizing how much of footwear product development is documentation and process discipline rather than design judgment alone — the tech pack and BOM material made clear that ambiguity is treated as a development failure, not a style choice. The sample-room-versus-mass-production distinction is the one I expect to matter most once I reach my own prototype: whatever construction I pick has to survive being explained to someone else and repeated, not just work once in my own hands.
